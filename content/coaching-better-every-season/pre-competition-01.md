# PRE-COMPETITION

## 84. Ensuring Pre-Competition Readiness

Any coach or athlete will tell you that competition days feel different.

There is additional pressure and distraction.

Performing in front of a crowd, family, and friends adds pressure, especially during important competitions.

Emotions may range from excitement to fear, particularly when facing a difficult opponent or when a great deal is at stake.

All the work completed in practice can be wasted if the coach neglects to prepare a detailed competition-day plan.

The focus of pre-competition activities should be helping athletes reach a point where they feel physically and mentally prepared for peak performance.

Several issues can significantly affect competitive performance:

* The final practice or walkthrough.
* Sleep and rest.
* Energy and nutrition requirements.
* The on-site warm-up.
* Mental and emotional preparation.
## 85. The Final Practice Walkthrough

The final practice before competition should be shorter than normal but remain high in intensity.

It should reinforce the competition strategy and address key performance skills and tactics specific to the upcoming event.

Be careful when explaining why certain phases of the game are being emphasized. Too much discussion of the opponent can build them up too much or not enough.

Walk through and rehearse logistical details.

Make sure everyone knows what to do.
## 86. Sleep and Rest

Lack of sleep is a risk factor for injury in sport.

The optimal amount of sleep is generally 8 to 10 hours per night.

Sleep supports repair and growth, calmness, recovery, and digestion.

Avoid screen time before sleep.

Screens and electronic devices reduce available sleep time and can make it more difficult to fall asleep.

During the hour before sleep, it is better to listen to music, read a book, talk with friends, or perform another calming activity.
## 87. Power Naps

A short afternoon nap can be helpful.

Many people feel tired in the early afternoon because of circadian rhythms.

Short naps are associated with improved focus and alertness during the day.

Coaches should practice taking brief power naps when appropriate to model and sustain physical and mental alertness.
## 88. Pre-Competition Nutrition

Food choices should be appropriate for both female and male athletes, while recognizing that female athletes may have unique energy demands.

Some athletes may have deficiencies in nutrients such as vitamin D or iron.

1. Eat familiar foods to avoid stomach problems before competition.
2. Drink water throughout the day. Do not wait until you feel thirsty. Aim for pale-colored urine.
3. Eat a larger meal approximately 3 to 4 hours before competition.
4. Eat a small snack approximately 1 to 3 hours before competition.
5. Consume sufficient carbohydrates.
6. Combine carbohydrates with appropriate foods such as salmon and vegetables when suitable.
7. Choose fresh fruits and vegetables when possible.
8. Avoid or limit foods that are very high in fat, protein, or fiber immediately before competition because they may cause digestive problems.

Foods with a high glycemic index are converted into available energy relatively quickly and may be useful during or after competition.

Low-glycemic foods take longer to digest and can provide energy over a longer period, making them appropriate for some pre-competition meals.

The glycemic index describes how quickly carbohydrate-containing foods affect blood sugar levels.
## 89. Guidelines for Ensuring Energy Needs on Competition Days

Eat carbohydrate-rich foods and vegetables that you are accustomed to.

Avoid unfamiliar spices.

Avoid fried foods and soft drinks.

Eat approximately the same amount as you would during normal meals.

Take your time and eat while seated.

Eat real food rather than highly processed substitutes. Choose foods that are as close as possible to their natural source.
## 90. The On-Field Pre-Game Warm-Up

The pre-game warm-up should include activities that prepare and activate athletes for fluid movement.

It is generally divided into three phases:

1. Light aerobic activities.
2. Stretching.
3. Sport-specific technical skills.

Each phase may last approximately 5 to 7 minutes, for a total of 15 to 20 minutes.

Stretching may be static or dynamic.

Static stretching involves holding a position without movement.

Dynamic stretching involves repetitive movements performed in a controlled manner.

For explosive sports, athletes should emphasize dynamic stretching.

For sports requiring a high degree of static flexibility, longer static holds may be appropriate.

Use full-body stretching while targeting the muscle groups that are most at risk in the specific sport.

All athletes should prepare their bodies regardless of sport or position.

Stretching time can also help athletes prepare mentally.
## 91. Pre-Competition Mental Preparation

Athletes should identify strategies that work for them personally.

The coach should help athletes learn how to monitor their pre-competition mental and emotional states.

The coach should also provide guidance for developing personalized and appropriate mental-preparation strategies.
## 92. Diaphragmatic Breathing

Diaphragmatic breathing is a simple and highly effective technique.

Under pressure, people often take short, shallow breaths that increase physical arousal and create tension in the shoulders and neck.

To counter this reaction, place one hand on the belly and the other on the chest.

Inhale slowly for 3 to 5 seconds, allowing the belly to expand, and then exhale fully.
## 93. Progressive Muscle Relaxation

This technique involves alternating between tightening and relaxing different muscle groups.

For example, tighten the arms and make a firm fist.

Hold the tension for 5 to 7 seconds, then release.
## 94. Visualization

Visualization is one of the most common techniques used by athletes and coaches to prepare mentally for competition.

Athletes should imagine what may happen, how they will feel, how loud the environment will be, how hot it will be, and other details of the competitive experience.

Imagery may have a stronger influence when combined with music.
## 95. Pre-Game Speeches

Emotional or inspirational speeches may be especially effective for underdog teams.

Tactical or instructional speeches may be most beneficial for athletes who already feel prepared.

Short videos and motivational quotations can be included as part of the pre-game message.

Highlight videos can help athletes feel confident and ready to meet the challenge of a strong opponent.

Lowlight videos may motivate athletes to prove that they are better than the performances shown.
## 96. John Wooden’s Approach to Pre-Game Speeches

John Wooden generally avoided pre-game speeches.

He believed that other teams might try to rise suddenly to a level they had not previously reached, while his own team should have already attained that level through preparation.

“We shall already have attained it in our preparation. We shall be there to begin with. A speech by me should not be necessary.”

Pre-game speeches can still be effective when they match the moment and the emotional needs of the athletes.
## 97. The Competition Checklist

A checklist simplifies complex tasks and helps athletes focus on what matters most.

Checklists are especially important when athletes travel to competition sites.

Remain flexible because the environment can change unexpectedly.

The checklist may include food, clothing, equipment, schedules, and other important items.

Coaches can sit with athletes and explain how to use the checklist.

Once an athlete is mature enough, they should prepare the checklist independently because they understand what works best for them.
