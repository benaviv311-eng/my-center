# PRESEASON — Purpose, Philosophy, and Team Culture

## 16. The Six Keys to Athlete Leadership

The first key is understanding that the team matters more than any individual. Personal interests should not come before the team’s shared purpose.

The second is recognizing that every role matters. Each athlete should understand that every job and contribution has value, regardless of status or playing time.

The third is treating everyone with respect. Leadership is demonstrated through consistent respect for teammates, coaches, opponents, staff, and officials.

The fourth is sharing victories and defeats. Win as a team and lose as a team. Do not claim personal credit after success or blame others after failure.

The fifth is accepting criticism. Leaders must receive constructive feedback without becoming defensive and use it as an opportunity to improve.

The sixth is keeping the coach informed. Athlete leaders should communicate relevant concerns, changes, and team issues before they become larger problems.

Leadership is not a reward for being the most talented player. It is a responsibility to serve the team.
## 17. Constructive Conflict Resolution

Resolving conflict constructively is an essential leadership skill.

Dysfunctional teams depend on the coach to solve every disagreement. Effective teams learn to communicate, listen, accept responsibility, and work through appropriate problems together.

The coach should not ignore conflict, but should teach athletes the skills needed to manage it.

The goal is to develop a team that can address disagreements maturely without requiring the coach to control every interaction.

Conflict can become an opportunity for growth when athletes remain respectful, separate facts from assumptions, listen to different perspectives, and search for solutions that serve the team’s shared purpose.
## 18. The Three Cs of Effective Communication

The first C is clarity. Messages, expectations, roles, and standards should be easy to understand.

The second C is consistency. Communication should remain aligned with the program’s core values and coaching philosophy. Words, decisions, and behaviour should reinforce the same message.

The third C is commonplace. Communication should become a normal and frequent part of team life—not something that occurs only when there is a problem.

Regular conversations make difficult discussions easier and prevent issues from accumulating.
## 19. Understanding the Four Stages of Team Dynamics

Teams generally develop through four stages: forming, storming, norming, and performing.

During the forming stage, new and returning athletes test the coach to understand the boundaries of acceptable and expected behaviour.

Athletes are searching for the group’s identity, values, and culture. The coach should provide clear guidance, consistent communication, opportunities for connection, and a strong introduction to the team’s purpose.

Storming is often the most emotional stage. Athletes are attempting to adapt to one another, their roles, the coach, and the program’s expectations.

Subgroups will naturally form because some athletes connect more easily with certain teammates. The coach does not need to eliminate these relationships but must ensure they do not divide the team or become more important than its shared purpose.

During storming, coaches should maintain the shared goal, select leaders who represent the program’s values, respond with understanding, gather complete information, and avoid impulsive assumptions.

Without effective leadership, a team may remain trapped in the storming stage.

During the norming stage, athletes begin accepting their roles and developing a “we” mentality.

This creates an opportunity to establish harmony, open communication, and honest sharing of opinions.

Two useful questions are: What is your influence on the team? And what is your influence on the game?

Collective goals, team-based rewards, clear roles, and recognition of less-visible contributions can strengthen this stage.

At the performing stage, the team’s energy and attention are invested in achieving its goals.

Coaches and athletes trust one another and are willing to take appropriate risks together. The team focuses on problem-solving, shared responsibility, continuous improvement, honest communication, and learning from mistakes.

Athletes and coaches should accept responsibility without judging, blaming, or humiliating one another.
## 20. Building the Team

Five principles guide effective team-building.

First, do not worry about personal credit.

Second, place the right person in the right role.

Third, create enough mutual respect for anyone to lead when the situation requires it.

Fourth, maintain clear and continuous communication.

Fifth, remember that the greatest achievements result from united effort and become more meaningful when shared with others.

The season can begin with a team evening, training camp, workshop, or shared challenge.

These activities can introduce the team’s goals and values, invite athlete feedback, observe communication and cooperation, and identify potential formal and informal leaders.

Team-building activities should have a clear developmental purpose rather than existing only as entertainment.
## 21. Strengthening Social and Task Cohesion

Cohesion is the degree to which a group feels connected and remains united while pursuing a shared purpose.

Social cohesion describes how strongly athletes feel personally connected to one another.

Task cohesion describes how strongly athletes are united by shared performance goals and responsibilities.

Both forms contribute to individual and team performance. Social connection may be particularly influential in some groups, but coaches should develop both social and task cohesion.

Strategies include consistently reinforcing team values, demonstrating genuine enthusiasm, establishing meaningful collective goals, using motivational stories, creating opportunities for cooperation, recognizing team-oriented behaviour, and encouraging open communication.

Appropriate humour and playful challenges can also strengthen relationships, but they should never become humiliation or exclusion.

Every strategy should align with the team’s values and the coach’s philosophy. The coach must remain authentic and be able to explain the purpose behind each method.
## 22. Increasing Collective Efficacy

Collective efficacy is the shared confidence of athletes and coaches in the team’s ability to meet the demands of intense practice and competition.

Teams with strong collective efficacy tend to demonstrate greater commitment, sustained effort, trust, cohesion, and confidence under pressure.

Collective efficacy has five important sources.

The first is past performance. Previous success provides evidence that the team can overcome challenges. Coaches should remind athletes of successful performances, improvement, and difficult situations the group has already handled.

The second is verbal persuasion. Credible encouragement from coaches, teammates, and respected leaders can strengthen confidence. Positive messages must be honest and supported by preparation rather than empty praise.

The third is vicarious experience. Watching teammates, previous teams, or similar groups succeed helps athletes believe that they can succeed as well.

The fourth is the motivational climate. An environment that emphasizes effort, learning, cooperation, and improvement builds shared confidence. Athletes should feel safe enough to make mistakes and take appropriate risks.

The fifth is team size. Team size affects communication, participation, role clarity, and connection. The group should be large enough to meet competitive demands but small enough for every athlete to feel visible and responsible.

Stories of past success, shared symbols, and team traditions can strengthen collective identity.

Success creates shared confidence, and shared confidence can increase the likelihood of future success.
## 23. Transformational Leadership

Transformational coaches inspire athletes to become self-directed, motivated, and committed to the team’s shared purpose.

Personal interests are placed beneath collective needs and goals. These coaches promote brotherhood, shared identity, personal example, athlete decision-making, and shared leadership.

Transformational leadership contains four elements.

The first is idealized influence. The coach models humility, integrity, modesty, and the team’s core values.

The second is inspirational motivation. The coach creates enthusiasm for shared goals and reinforces progress positively.

The third is intellectual stimulation. Athletes are encouraged to think independently, solve problems, be creative, and take appropriate risks.

The fourth is individualized consideration. The coach understands each athlete’s needs and provides individualized attention, support, and challenges.

Transformational leadership can develop confidence, cohesion, enjoyment, independence, and concern for others. It gives athletes meaningful opportunities to participate in decisions and leadership.

However, leadership style alone does not guarantee success. Athletes’ expectations, commitment, preparation, and belief in what the group can achieve also influence competitive performance.
## 24. Servant Leadership

Servant leadership is centred on others and places great importance on integrity, empathy, morality, and care.

The coach leads by serving athletes and helping them develop. The primary question is not how athletes can serve the coach’s ambitions, but how the coach can help athletes grow.

Servant leaders build trusting relationships, demonstrate compassion and humility, include athletes in appropriate decisions, maintain ethical standards, recognize individual strengths, and enable athletes rather than controlling them.

Athletes coached in this way may experience greater satisfaction, cohesion, enjoyment, trust, and respect for the coach.

Transformational and servant leadership share a central purpose: helping athletes meet their technical, physical, psychological, and emotional needs.

Transformational leadership emphasizes a shared vision and collective change. Servant leadership places greater emphasis on care, service, integrity, and individual well-being.

Effective coaching begins with compassion for the people the coach has been trusted to lead. Respect, appreciation, and care must be demonstrated consistently through daily actions.
## 25. Balancing Demands and Compassion

Athlete-centred leadership requires a careful balance between challenging athletes to approach their limits and providing the compassion and support they need to develop.

Being athlete-centred does not mean lowering standards or avoiding discipline.

Effective coaches combine positive reinforcement with accountability, compassion with toughness, clear boundaries with shared leadership, competitive intensity with healthy relationships, and permission to make mistakes with responsibility to learn from them.

Athletes should be allowed to make mistakes without dwelling on them. The coach’s response should help them understand what happened, identify the next action, and move forward.

An athlete-centred environment includes clear rules, consistent discipline, trust, inclusion, psychological safety, healthy internal competition, opportunities for shared leadership, and a commitment to effort and improvement.

Athletes are more likely to enjoy, learn, and perform well when they feel trusted, included, supported, and appropriately challenged.

The exact name given to a leadership style is less important than the coach’s awareness of their core values and ability to express them consistently.

The central question is not: What kind of leader do I call myself?

The central question is: Do my daily decisions and actions reflect the values and athlete-centred philosophy I claim to follow?
