# PRESEASON — Purpose, Philosophy, and Team Culture

## 7. Communicating the Coaching Philosophy

A philosophy becomes valuable when athletes, staff members, and parents can understand it and recognize it in the coach’s behaviour.

Storytelling is a powerful method of communicating a philosophy because stories connect with both logic and emotion. They transform abstract values into experiences that people can understand and remember.

There are three useful types of stories.

Stories of self describe personal experiences that explain who the coach is, what the coach believes, and how the philosophy developed.

Stories of the group describe shared experiences that strengthen team identity, belonging, and collective responsibility.

Stories of values and meaning demonstrate why the team’s purpose, standards, and values matter.

The relationship between individual and collective strength can be communicated through the idea that the strength of the pack is the wolf, and the strength of the wolf is the pack. Individuals strengthen the team, while the team supports and strengthens every individual.

A philosophy is expressed most powerfully through behaviour rather than words. Athletes will judge it by how the coach communicates, responds under pressure, handles mistakes, distributes opportunities, and treats people.

Parents should also be introduced to the philosophy through meetings or other communication channels. When they understand its purpose and emotional meaning, they are more likely to feel connected to the process and support the team’s direction.
## 8. Vision, Targets, and Goal Setting

The preseason should begin with an important question: What kind of season would make us look back with pride and feel that all the time and energy were worthwhile?

A meaningful vision describes what the program can become and what kind of experience it wants to create.

Target outcomes provide the destination. Vision provides the energy for the journey. Target conditions define the behaviours and mindset required to close the gap between the present position and the desired destination.

Effective goals should be SMART.

They should be specific, meaning clearly written and defined.

They should be measurable, meaning observable and possible to track.

They should be attainable, meaning supported by the available resources.

They should be realistic, meaning appropriate for the athlete’s current ability and circumstances.

They should be time-bound, meaning connected to a clear completion or review date.

Goals should be ambitious but realistic. They should address both individual and team development and be understood by athletes and coaches alike.

Goals should be written in positive terms, include short- and long-term objectives, and explain how the desired outcome will be achieved. They should also be evaluated and adjusted as reality changes.

Unrealistically high goals can damage confidence, motivation, and team cohesion. Appropriate shared goals encourage athletes to play better together and work harder for one another.

There are three types of goals.

Outcome goals describe the final result the athlete or team wants to achieve. These provide direction but are not entirely within the team’s control.

Performance goals establish measurable standards that are less dependent on the opponent.

Process goals identify the specific actions, habits, and behaviours required to improve performance.

Outcome goals identify where the team wants to go. Performance and process goals explain how it will get there.
## 9. Program Targets and Athlete Targets

The best coaches are visionaries. They see what the program can become and create positive experiences that optimize athlete learning.

Four priorities can guide the program.

The first is participation. The program should maximize meaningful participation while considering available facilities, staff, resources, and practical roster size.

The second is retention. Coaches should inspire athletes to return each year. A target such as retaining seventy-five percent of the roster may be valuable because continuity helps preserve the team’s culture, identity, and traditions.

The third is engagement. The program should create experiences that keep athletes involved and support meaningful learning. Engagement can be evaluated by asking athletes to reflect on their experience at the end of the season.

The fourth is competitiveness. Coaches should establish realistic and adjustable performance targets that allow the team to compete consistently. These should focus on measurable standards the team can influence directly.

Athlete goals should be both realistic and courageous. Ask athletes what they are capable of achieving, what they want to achieve, and what they will need to do to reach it.

A simple preseason questionnaire can ask athletes to list three goals for the season, identify potential barriers that could prevent the team from achieving those goals, and list three things they must personally contribute.

The questionnaire gives the coach insight into each athlete’s ambitions, sense of responsibility, and perception of possible problems.

After athletes complete it, the coach and athlete should review the answers together and adjust the goals when necessary.

Goal-setting should remain an ongoing conversation. The frequency of progress reviews should depend on the type of goal. Process goals may require frequent monitoring, while long-term outcomes can be reviewed at broader intervals.

The greatest value of goals is not merely identifying a future result. Goals provide a daily reminder of the effort, attention, and behaviour required to improve.
## 10. Building Trust and Team Cohesion

The quality of a team depends heavily on the strength of the relationships among its athletes and coaches. Building relationships and creating a positive atmosphere should therefore be a central coaching priority.

Athletes are more likely to reach their potential when they trust one another, feel connected, and know that the team is a psychologically safe environment.

Mutual trust allows people to reveal their fears, weaknesses, needs, and vulnerabilities without expecting ridicule, judgment, or hidden motives.

Trust enables athletes to direct their energy toward the team’s goals instead of protecting themselves from teammates.

Trust does not develop automatically simply because people practise and compete together. It requires time, consistency, and shared experiences.

Information athletes share privately should be treated respectfully and confidentially, except when their safety or well-being requires the coach to involve an appropriate responsible person.
## 11. The Coaching Trust Bank

Coaches should make regular and systematic deposits into their relationships with athletes.

Trust deposits include making time to listen, showing genuine interest in athletes’ lives, keeping promises, following through, treating athletes fairly, protecting their dignity, offering honest support, and admitting mistakes when appropriate.

Every interaction can either strengthen or weaken the balance in the coaching trust bank.

A large trust balance gives the coach greater freedom to challenge the athlete, provide difficult feedback, and manage demanding situations without immediately damaging the relationship.

Trust cannot be demanded. It must be earned gradually through reliable behaviour.
## 12. The 3+1 Coach–Athlete Relationship Model

The 3+1 model describes four dimensions of the coach-athlete relationship: closeness, commitment, complementarity, and co-orientation.

Closeness describes mutual respect, trust, appreciation, and emotional connection.

It can be supported through encouragement and appropriate positive gestures, such as high-fives or a welcome pat on the back. Any physical contact must be respectful, appropriate, and comfortable for the athlete.

Commitment is the conscious decision to invest time and energy in maintaining the relationship. It represents the coach’s and athlete’s desire to continue working together and supporting the developmental process.

Complementarity describes the ability of the coach and athlete to cooperate effectively in pursuit of a shared goal.

It requires empathy and compassion because both sides must consider different perspectives and discuss disagreements in a friendly and constructive way.

Co-orientation refers to the extent to which the coach and athlete understand and agree with each other’s perspectives.

For example, it examines whether both people have the same understanding of their level of commitment. Trust is strengthened when what the coach believes about the relationship matches what the athlete actually experiences.
## 13. The PITCH Communication Framework

The PITCH framework describes five qualities of optimal communication.

The first is productive. Coaches should keep their emotions under control and communicate positively, efficiently, and with a clear purpose.

The second is informational. Feedback should be specific, relevant, and useful to the athlete.

The third is timed. Feedback should be delivered as close as reasonably possible to the behaviour or event, while allowing time for emotions to settle when necessary.

The fourth is consistent. Communication should align with the team’s core values. Verbal messages, body language, decisions, and actions should communicate the same standard.

The fifth is honest. Coaches should communicate directly without hidden messages, manipulation, or mind games.

Trust and cohesion are created when athletes repeatedly experience communication that is productive, informational, timely, consistent, and honest.
## 14. Identifying Athletes’ Best Roles

Leadership responsibilities should be shared with athletes rather than held entirely by the coaching staff.

Possible roles include team captain, practice leader, social-events coordinator, team-culture leader, communication representative, equipment manager, or mentor for younger athletes.

Roles should reflect each athlete’s strengths, personality, maturity, interests, credibility, and ability to serve the team.

Athletic talent does not automatically create leadership ability. When coaches assign athletes leadership positions, they must also teach them how to lead, communicate, manage conflict, make decisions, and model the team’s values.

The purpose of sharing responsibility is not to reduce the coach’s workload alone. It is to develop ownership, strengthen the team, and prepare athletes to contribute beyond their playing performance.
## 15. Coach Communication Guidelines

Effective communication should be direct. If athletes do not understand the coach, they cannot know what is expected or where the coach stands.

Coaches should own their messages by using statements such as “I think,” “I feel,” or “I need,” rather than presenting personal interpretations as universal facts.

Messages should be complete and specific. Athletes need enough information to understand the message and take appropriate action.

Communication should be clear and consistent. Coaches should not say one thing and do another. Words, standards, decisions, and behaviour should communicate the same message.

Coaches should clearly express their needs and feelings. Appropriate emotional honesty can strengthen understanding and connection.

Facts should be separated from opinions. Begin with what was directly observed or is known, and only then explain the interpretation.

Each message should focus on one subject. Moving between several unrelated issues will confuse the athlete.

Important concerns should be addressed promptly rather than allowed to accumulate. However, the coach should wait when strong emotions could distort judgment.

Messages should have no hidden agenda. The stated message should match its real purpose.

Communication should be supportive and avoid threats, sarcasm, negative comparisons, humiliation, and personal judgment. Correct the behaviour while protecting the athlete’s dignity.

Verbal and nonverbal communication should be congruent. Tone, facial expression, posture, and body language should support the spoken message.

Important messages should be reinforced. Repeat central points and communicate them through different methods when necessary, but avoid unnecessary nagging.

Communication should match the athlete’s level. Consider age, experience, background, and familiarity with technical language.

The coach should check for understanding rather than assume it. Ask the athlete to explain the instruction in their own words or demonstrate it.

The coach must first capture the athlete’s attention. Using the athlete’s name and explaining why the information matters can help.

Different teaching methods should be combined, including visual demonstrations, verbal explanations, guided questions, physical practice, and feedback during performance.

The objective is not to label athletes according to one fixed learning style. It is to find the method, or combination of methods, that helps each athlete understand and apply the information.
