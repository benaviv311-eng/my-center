# OFF-SEASON — Reflection and Coach Development

## 155. Recharge: Preventing Coaching Burnout

Coaching can become an all-consuming lifestyle.

Its emotional, mental, and physical demands can exhaust even highly accomplished and resilient coaches.

Recognizing the causes of stress and making time to recharge are essential for preventing burnout.

### Five Major Sources of Coaching Stress and Burnout

1. **Communication and Conflict**

Miscommunication or conflict with administrators, parents, athletes, and staff.

2. **Pressure and Expectations**

Unrealistic performance targets, being evaluated solely according to athlete results, self-imposed pressure, and a lack of job security.

3. **Athlete Discipline and Team Dynamics**

Poor commitment, effort, focus, or character.

Problems with team cohesion, role acceptance, and playing time.

Difficult decisions involving recruitment, selection, or removing athletes from the team.

4. **Insufficient Support and Isolation**

Inadequate support for the sports program or for the coach’s personal and professional development.

5. **Personal Sacrifice**

Heavy workloads and conflicting family demands can strain relationships and reduce personal time.

A lack of sleep, recovery, and regular physical activity may also damage the coach’s health.
## 156. Resilience, Recovery, and Mindfulness

A hardy personality helps coaches view difficulties and setbacks as challenges rather than threats.

Resilient coaches do not avoid pressure. They develop the capacity to recover from it and continue functioning effectively.

However, constant stress can eventually weaken even a highly resilient coach.

Losing, criticism, uncertainty, long working hours, and the feeling that coaching continues twenty-four hours a day can gradually reduce the coach’s physical and emotional resources.

The off-season should therefore include genuine recovery.

Recovery is not a reward for finishing the season. It is an essential part of preparation for the next one.

Coaches should create time for sleep, physical activity, reflection, family, enjoyable activities, and emotional recovery.

Daily reflection and self-care should continue throughout the year rather than beginning only after signs of burnout appear.

Coaches should also learn how to transition between roles.

When practice or competition ends, the coach may need to shift intentionally into the role of spouse, parent, friend, or family member.

Without this transition, coaching pressure can follow the coach into every other part of life.

### Four Mindset Questions

1. Why do I coach?
2. Why do I coach the way I do?
3. What does it feel like to be coached by me?
4. How do I define success?

Mindfulness helps coaches notice their thoughts, feelings, and physical reactions without immediately judging or acting on them.

Positive self-talk can also support recovery.

Instead of treating a setback as proof of failure, the coach can reframe it as information, a temporary difficulty, or an opportunity to learn.
## 157. Strategies for a Resilient Coaching Career

Resilience should be developed deliberately throughout a coaching career.

### Recover After Difficult Experiences

Allow time to process losses, conflict, disappointment, and criticism.

Do not carry every difficult experience directly into the next practice, competition, or family interaction.

### Maintain Daily Self-Care

Protect sleep, physical activity, nutrition, reflection, and time away from coaching.

Small daily recovery habits are more effective than waiting until complete exhaustion occurs.

### Separate Coaching from Personal Identity

Coaching is an important role, but it is not the coach’s entire identity.

Results should be evaluated honestly without allowing wins and losses to determine personal worth.

### Transition Between Roles

Create a deliberate routine for moving from coaching into family and personal life.

This may include changing clothes, walking, breathing, writing brief notes, or completing a short reflection before leaving the coaching environment.

### Practise Mindfulness

Notice thoughts and emotions without automatically reacting to them.

Pause before responding during stressful interactions.

### Use Positive Self-Talk and Reframing

Replace destructive internal messages with realistic and constructive ones.

Focus on what can be controlled and identify the next useful action.

### Build Supportive Relationships

Maintain relationships with people who provide honest feedback, emotional support, perspective, and encouragement.

A resilient coaching career is not built alone.
## 158. Improving Communication and Overcoming Expectancy Bias

The way coaches communicate with athletes, and the way athletes communicate with coaches, are essential aspects of effective coaching.

The coach–athlete relationship is an interpersonal relationship.

Like every other relationship, it can be strengthened through constructive communication or damaged through destructive communication.

Destructive communication includes:

- Personal attacks.
- Sarcasm.
- Ridicule.
- Defensiveness.
- Contempt.
- Ignoring or denying another person’s perspective.

Destructive communication may also appear more subtly.

For example, a coach may communicate high expectations to certain athletes while communicating lower expectations to others.

These differences can influence athlete confidence, motivation, opportunities, and performance.

Expertise and experience can create a blind spot in understanding an athlete’s perspective.

As coaches gain experience, they become more confident in their own views and may increasingly see the world only from their own perspective.

However, effective communication requires an understanding of both people in the coach–athlete relationship.

When coaches assume that they know more than their athletes, they may rely heavily on commands and tell athletes what to do without first understanding what the athletes see, feel, or need.

The coach may believe that their perspective is objectively correct, while the athlete feels that the coach simply does not understand.

To overcome expectancy bias, coaches should examine the expectations they hold for each athlete.

They should ask questions, listen carefully, check for understanding, and remain open to perspectives that differ from their own.
## 159. Four Steps for a Fresh Outlook

1. Notice any thoughts or emotions that arise when you encounter the person.
2. Ask whether you are seeing the person as they are now or through a preconceived impression.
3. Remember that every person is unique, and avoid prejudging them.
4. Before making assumptions, ask clarifying questions and consider alternative explanations for their behaviour or response.

### Gratitude Note

Write a personal note to each athlete describing something you appreciated or felt grateful for during the previous season.

This can help you approach every athlete with a more positive, balanced, and open perspective.
## 160. Creating a Positive Team Environment

No individual is bigger than the team.

Individual brilliance does not automatically produce outstanding team results.

One selfish mindset can infect an entire team culture.

### Establish Clear Expectations

Create clear guidelines for team selection and written expectations for athlete behaviour.

Define the kind of person you are looking for when selecting athletes and staff.

Desired qualities may include:

- A desire to play.
- A desire to win.
- A willingness to work hard.
- A willingness to learn.
- A desire to improve.
- A willingness to compete.

Provide a clear description and practical examples of what each quality looks like.

Create a simple one-page document containing athlete expectations and behaviour standards.

The coach should clarify what they are committed to providing and what the athletes are expected to contribute.

The coach and athletes can sign this agreement to demonstrate their commitment.

### Build the Right Staff

Hire good people and many problems will be reduced before they begin.

Learn to trust the people you hire.

Delegate program responsibilities appropriately.

Delegation helps prevent coaching burnout and supports the development of a healthy and productive team environment.
## 161. Building a Strong Support System

Sustained coaching requires focus and energy.

A strong coaching network provides the emotional support needed to remain energized and ready to lead.

### Choose “Energy Givers”

Energy givers add value to your life and help refill your emotional reserves.

They:

- Support and inspire others while also renewing their own energy.
- Model a service-oriented attitude.
- Offer encouragement without avoiding accountability.
- Practise tough love by combining genuine care with honest challenges.

### Build Trusting Relationships

Trust allows coaches to challenge one another, discuss weaknesses openly, and remain receptive to change.

Look for people who:

- Give frequent, genuine, and truthful praise.
- Identify weaknesses instead of offering blind or undeserved approval.
- Listen carefully and seek to understand.
- Provide honest feedback and reliable emotional support.
- Serve as meaningful sources of inspiration.

The best support system does not simply make coaches feel comfortable.

It helps them grow.
## 162. Establishing Work–Life Balance

Coaching is not limited to the time spent on the court or field.

Planning, communication, recruitment, evaluation, problem-solving, travel, and emotional investment can allow coaching to occupy every part of life.

Without clear boundaries, coaches may remain mentally engaged with coaching even when they are physically at home.

Work–life balance does not mean giving equal time to every responsibility.

It means making deliberate choices about where time, attention, and energy are invested.

### Priority List

Identify the people, roles, and activities that matter most.

Protect focused work periods while leaving room for family, health, rest, recovery, and play.

Aim for sustainable effort rather than constant urgency.

Set boundaries that preserve energy for the commitments that matter most.

A coaching career should support a meaningful life rather than consume it.
