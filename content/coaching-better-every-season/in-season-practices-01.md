# IN-SEASON — Designing Effective Practices

## 58. Creating an Effective Practice Environment

Games and championships are won in practice first.

In general, athletes are inactive for nearly half of every practice. Much of the available time is spent proving what they can already do instead of addressing the more difficult task of improving their weaknesses.

A lack of attention and excessive fixation can lead to boredom and discipline problems. Keep this in mind when selecting every activity included in practice.
## 59. Designing Quality Practices

Learning how to design quality practices is one of the surest ways to increase coaching effectiveness and athlete success.

Quality practice design includes four factors:

1. Set challenging and specific practice goals.
2. Keep athletes physically and mentally active throughout practice.
3. Give athletes choices and ask for their input on practice design.
4. Create competitive, game-like practice activities.
## 60. Challenging and Specific Practice Goals

Set a goal for every practice. Every drill should have a specific purpose.

Learning is optimized when practice goals stretch athletes just beyond their current comfort zone.

Challenging but attainable goals, combined with clearly communicated high expectations, are one of the best ways to avoid wasting valuable practice time and dealing with discipline issues.

The purpose of practice is to stretch athletes just beyond their current skill level. When that happens, mistakes will be made.

From the outside, this may look like an ugly or disorganized practice. However, when mistakes are recognized and addressed appropriately, they become stepping stones on the path to success.
## 61. Ego Goals and Mastery Goals

Ego Goals: The emphasis is on avoiding mistakes and judging success by defeating someone else.

Mastery Goals: The emphasis is on developing ability, improving behavior, and becoming better than before.

Coaches who use mastery goals are often perceived by athletes as better at teaching and listening.

Write practice goals that clearly specify the behaviors and conditions that must be met for performance to be considered successful.

Mastery focuses on behavior. Mastery goals help athletes develop a growth mindset.

People with a growth mindset see mistakes as opportunities to improve. Failures are fertilizer for deeper learning and improved performance.

They view ability as something that can be improved and controlled through effortful and deliberate practice.

Failure is just one more try on the way to success.

Add the word “yet” to the end of self-critical statements.

Mastery goals teach athletes to focus on improving their ability rather than proving their ability.

This approach has a long-term effect on how athletes deal with plateaus or temporary declines in performance.

Communicate challenging practice goals before practice begins. Explaining the “why” gives athletes motivation. Tell them why an activity is important and how it will help them in the future.

Sharing the “why” has two main benefits:

1. Athletes value the activity more and put greater effort into it.
2. It builds trust and strengthens the coach-athlete relationship.

High expectations without appropriate support and guidance can severely demoralize athletes, increase their fear of punishment, erode trust, and damage the coach-athlete relationship.
## 62. Keeping Athletes Mentally and Physically Active

Practice sessions that are too long can lead to loss of focus and an increased risk of injury.

Focus on quality. Aim for short, high-intensity practice sessions.

Ask yourself: How can I use the least amount of practice time possible in the most efficient way?

Long practices are neither desirable nor practical.

Taking a long-term view of athlete development eliminates the need for coaches to feel that they must cover everything in a single session.

The optimal length of practice is determined by factors such as age, skill level, training experience, and the goals of the session.

Set two things before practice begins:

1. The number of practice sessions and their length.
2. The structure of practice, designed to maximize engagement and learning.

Serious coaches should expect to spend almost as much time designing a practice as running it.

Always prepare a detailed practice plan.
## 63. John Wooden’s Eight Principles of Practice Planning

1. Fundamentals before creativity.
2. Use variety.
3. Teach new material at the start of practice.
4. Make quick transitions between activities.
5. Increase complexity from practice to practice.
6. Continue the learning process throughout the season.
7. End on a positive note.
8. Avoid altering the plan during practice.
## 64. Teaching Basic Skills First

Basic skills provide the foundation for athletic creativity.

Repetition builds general motor programs.

Regardless of the athletes’ age or skill level, some practice time should always be set aside for learning or reinforcing basic skills.

Reinforcing basic skills can also build athletes’ confidence in their ability.
## 65. Using Variety

Regularly change activities to keep interest high.

Varied practice, or random practice, promotes perceptual attunement. It develops the athlete’s ability to make adjustments based on changes in the performance environment.

Select a theme for each practice and design activities that are connected to that theme.
## 66. Teaching New Material at the Start of Practice

During the first part of practice, usually the first 10 to 30 minutes, athletes are often most receptive to learning.

Using small-sided games and aerobic exercises can stimulate the release of chemicals in the brain that support learning.

Dopamine is an important neurotransmitter that can increase focus and strengthen the learning of new concepts.
## 67. Making Quick Transitions Between Activities

Time spent moving from one activity to the next should be minimized.

Have athletes move quickly from one drill to another to keep practice moving, maintain intensity, and reduce wasted time.

Prepare the equipment in advance.

Make sure players understand the practice routines and progressions.

Ask players to fill their water bottles before practice.

A group break for everyone at the same time can cause distractions and loss of focus. When possible, integrate water breaks into the practice structure.
## 68. Increasing Complexity from Practice to Practice

At the fundamental stage, use blocked practice.

Once athletes become comfortable performing the skill, gradually increase the complexity.

Add layers of difficulty over time rather than trying to rush toward perfection.
## 69. Progressing Practice Throughout the Season

The first few weeks of practice each season should be approached as a time to prepare athletes for increasingly complex and high-intensity sessions.

After conditioning has been developed, late-season practices should be shortened to keep athletes fresh.
## 70. Ending on a Positive Note

Set aside time for silly and enjoyable activities as a contrast to deliberate practice.

To keep motivation high, allow athletes to choose the final activity of the workout.

At the end of practice, give athletes time to express themselves. They may correct a mistake they made during practice, share a reflection, say something funny, or mention something meaningful they took from the session.
## 71. Avoiding Alterations to the Plan During Practice

If later practice activities are shortened or eliminated, athletes lose opportunities to practice skills the coach previously believed were critical.

If an activity is extended too long, athletes may lose focus because they do not know how long they will be participating. This can also make them more susceptible to injury.
## 72. Practical Principles for Efficient Practice

1. Never extend practice beyond the scheduled time.
2. Never schedule practices longer than two hours.
3. Limit activities to approximately 1 to 8 minutes when appropriate.
4. Integrate water breaks into drills.
5. Reduce transition time between activities.
6. Provide athletes with opportunities to express individuality and creativity.
7. Balance coach-designed structure with athlete choice.

Balancing coach-designed structure with athlete choice is an effective way to develop athletic talent.
## 73. Three Basic Psychological Needs

Three basic needs enhance motivation and relationships:

1. The need for choice and autonomy.
2. The need to learn and feel competent.
3. The need to feel connected to others.

The opposite of autonomy-supportive coaching is controlling coaching.

A controlling approach focuses on directing athletes as a way to motivate and teach them. Although it may produce short-term performance gains, it can reduce motivation over the long term.
## 74. Pull and Ask

Pull and Ask: Use frequent questioning, listening, and paraphrasing.

This approach encourages athletes to think, reflect, and take ownership of their learning.

An autonomy-supportive approach to practice design has been shown to be effective with adolescents and youth-sport athletes.

Athletes should be given opportunities to perform skills in ways that feel right to them.

The coach and athlete can then jointly determine the most efficient way to perform the skill, or keep multiple effective options available.

Coaches who use an autonomy-supportive approach are often perceived by athletes as more organized, more intense, and more invested in athlete learning.
## 75. Push and Tell

Push and Tell: This approach relies mainly on direct instruction and coach advice.

The coach controls the learning process by telling athletes what to do instead of helping them discover and understand solutions independently.

Direct instruction is sometimes necessary, but it should be balanced with questioning, listening, and athlete involvement.
## 76. Practical Autonomy-Supportive Coaching

Give athletes free time during practice to work on their strengths and weaknesses.

Let them choose an exercise from a prepared list.

Explain the benefits of the method being taught.

Listen openly and without judgment.

Collect feedback during practice and use it to adjust future sessions.

Actively seek feedback about the practice so you can understand how athletes experience your coaching.

Recognize that there may be more than one effective way to perform a skill.

Do not use ultimatums or shame athletes to get what you want.
## 77. Creating Competitive, Game-Like Practice Activities

Mimic competition in practice as much as possible.

Create drills that are physically and emotionally challenging.

Make practice as game-like as possible.
## 78. The Playing-Form Approach

Create practice activities that simulate competitive, game-like conditions.

Athletes learn through small-sided games that provide a more authentic and challenging learning experience.

Make sure each athlete remains active as much as possible.

The competitive, game-like approach gives athletes important opportunities to perform in a controlled environment while still reading and reacting to opponent pressure, speed, and decision-making demands.
## 79. The Drill Approach

Isolate and restrict complex game skills.

Break skills into stages and teach specific techniques without the pressure of a real game.

This approach is useful for learning fundamentals and correcting technique.

It is the opposite of the playing-form approach, although effective practices usually include a combination of both.
## 80. The Teaching-Grid Approach

Divide the practice area into multiple activity areas.

Split athletes into groups, with each group performing a different activity.

This can provide athletes with many more touches on the ball than a traditional full-team drill.

The goal is constant movement, active involvement, and game-like pressure.
## 81. Three Stages for Designing Game-Like Activities

1. Shaping play.
2. Focusing play.
3. Enhancing play.
## 82. Shaping Play

Change the rules of the game, alter the number of players, or modify the size of the playing area.

During drills that simulate game situations, provide guiding instructions when necessary.

Use ball-in activities and avoid mindless conditioning.

All tactical drills should include both offensive and defensive components.

Place game-like activities near the end of practice as a way to finish with something fun and competitive.

Each practice will likely include a mixture of traditional drills and game-like activities.

The optimal balance depends on age, skill level, and practice goals.
## 83. Four Features of Effective Practice

1. Set challenging and specific practice goals.
2. Keep athletes physically and mentally active throughout practice.
3. Give athletes choices and ask for their input on practice design.
4. Create competitive, game-like practice activities.
