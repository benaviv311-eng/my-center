# IN-SEASON — Athlete Development and Learning

## 36. How Athletes Learn

Learning contains three central components: process, change, and experience.

Process refers to the activities and experiences that lead toward learning.

Change refers to meaningful development in knowledge, understanding, behaviour, or performance.

Experience refers to the previous and current experiences through which learning occurs.

Each athlete experiences the learning process differently. Coaches must observe how individuals understand, organize, and apply information.

Learning is not demonstrated only by immediate improvement during practice.

True learning involves a relatively stable change that athletes can retain and apply later, including under pressure or in a different environment.
## 37. Prior Knowledge and Chunking

Athletes do not arrive as blank slates. They bring previous knowledge, habits, beliefs, emotions, and experiences.

Prior experience can accelerate learning by providing a useful foundation. It can also interfere with learning when previous habits conflict with the new skill.

Before teaching something new, coaches should discover what athletes already know.

Ask athletes to explain the skill, demonstrate their current version, describe similar skills, complete a brief self-assessment, or identify what they believe they need to improve.

Novices commonly organize information sequentially. They consciously think through each step in a fixed order.

Experts organize related information into meaningful clusters through a process known as chunking.

Instead of processing every detail separately, experts recognize familiar patterns and respond to them as complete units.

Chunking helps experts analyse situations and perform skills more quickly, efficiently, and accurately.

Coaches should help athletes gradually connect isolated details into meaningful movement and tactical patterns.
## 38. Priming Athletes for Learning

Priming occurs when exposure to a cue influences the response to a related activity that follows.

Words, images, sensations, demonstrations, metaphors, and environmental cues can activate thoughts, emotions, and movement qualities that support learning and performance.

Examples include “move as quickly as a cheetah,” “be explosive like a gorilla,” “let the movement flow,” or “perform it smoothly and automatically.”

Effective cues should create a clear image without requiring a long technical explanation.

Coaches can introduce the central image or idea before practice and repeat it immediately before the activity.

Because the immediate effect may be brief, cues should be reinforced close to performance rather than mentioned once and forgotten.

Priming can be combined with mental imagery by asking athletes what the movement will look like, what it will feel like, which part will require attention, and how they will respond in a specific situation.

New skills are also easier to understand when coaches connect them to familiar movements or tactical situations.

Analogies and consistent teaching structures help athletes build richer knowledge and recognize recurring patterns.
## 39. Understanding Motivation and the Learning Process

Athlete motivation to learn is shaped by two primary questions.

The first is: Does this learning activity matter to me?

The second is: Do I believe I can succeed at it?

Motivation is strongest when athletes understand the value of an activity and believe that success is possible through effort, effective strategies, and appropriate support.

Coaches should explain why an activity matters—not only what athletes must do.

They should also provide experiences that strengthen the athlete’s belief that improvement is possible.
## 40. The Three Sources of Value

The first source is attainment value.

Attainment value comes from personal satisfaction, successful performance, and the need to feel competent.

Coaches can develop it by beginning practice with activities that have a high probability of success, occasionally repeating previously successful activities, showing athletes evidence of improvement, and connecting success to effort and effective strategies.

The second source is intrinsic value.

Intrinsic value comes from enjoyment, curiosity, challenge, and love of the sport.

Coaches should create engaging activities that are slightly beyond the athlete’s current ability but still perceived as achievable.

This creates the learning sweet spot and favourable conditions for concentration, enjoyment, and moments of flow.

The third source is instrumental value.

Instrumental value comes from an external benefit or reward associated with completing a learning activity successfully.

Examples include award shirts, small prizes, team privileges, public recognition, or waiving a planned conditioning activity when athletes achieve a predetermined success rate.

Rewards should reinforce specific behaviours, effort, determination, improvement, and team values.

Athletes should understand exactly what they did to earn the recognition.
## 41. Expectancy Beliefs

Expectancy beliefs describe whether athletes believe they can successfully complete a learning activity.

Coaches can strengthen these beliefs by setting challenging but achievable tasks, dividing difficult skills into manageable steps, providing clear demonstrations, reminding athletes of previous success, and showing how effort and effective strategies produce improvement.

The objective is not to convince athletes that every task will be easy.

It is to help them believe that progress is possible and that they have a realistic pathway toward success.

Confidence should be supported by evidence, preparation, and meaningful achievement rather than empty praise.
## 42. The Zone of Proximal Development

The zone of proximal development is the space between what athletes can already accomplish independently and what they cannot yet accomplish alone but can achieve with appropriate guidance or support.

The most productive activities are just beyond the athlete’s current independent ability while remaining within reach.

An activity may be too easy when athletes appear bored, lose concentration, or succeed without meaningful effort.

It may be too difficult when they repeatedly experience confusion, frustration, failure, or complete loss of effective technique.

The challenge is appropriate when athletes demonstrate concentration, manageable mistakes, responsiveness to feedback, and visible improvement.

The learning sweet spot is individual. The same task may be too easy for one athlete and overwhelming for another.

Simple and regular feedback from athletes can help the coach adjust the activity’s difficulty and the amount of support provided.
## 43. Teaching Component Skills

Before teaching a target skill, coaches should identify every component that influences the athlete’s ability to perform it successfully.

Components may include basic technique, explosive strength, flexibility, mobility, agility, balance, coordination, rotational ability, perception, decision-making, and tactical understanding.

Coaches often overlook these components because they assume athletes already possess the necessary foundations.

When training becomes more advanced and focuses heavily on tactics or playing systems, basic technical weaknesses may be neglected and eventually limit future progress.

A practical process is to define the target skill, break it into component skills, assess the athlete’s ability in each component, identify the component limiting performance, develop it, and then reintegrate it into the complete skill.

When an athlete struggles, the coach should not simply repeat the entire instruction. The coach should identify and rebuild the missing foundation.
## 44. The Three Stages of Skill Mastery

The first stage is the mental stage.

The athlete consciously thinks about how to perform the skill and attempts to understand its important steps.

The second stage is the practice stage.

The athlete uses previous knowledge, repetition, reflection, and feedback to refine and improve the skill.

The third stage is the automatic stage.

The athlete performs the skill more reliably, efficiently, and with less conscious effort.

The progression can be summarized as: think, refine, perform.

Automatic performance does not mean learning has ended. The athlete must continue adapting the skill to different environments, opponents, speeds, pressures, and tactical demands.
## 45. The Four Stages of Competence

The first stage is unconscious incompetence.

Athletes cannot yet perform the skill and are unaware of what they need to learn.

The second stage is conscious incompetence.

Athletes understand what they should do but cannot yet perform it successfully.

The third stage is conscious competence.

Athletes can perform the skill but must consciously think through many of its steps.

The fourth stage is unconscious competence.

After extensive and focused practice, athletes can perform the skill proficiently with little conscious attention.

Coaches should teach according to the athlete’s current stage—not according to how the skill feels to the coach.
## 46. The Expert Blind Spot

The expert blind spot occurs when experts overlook fundamental steps because those steps have become easy and automatic for them.

Coaches may have spent thousands of hours developing a skill as athletes. When teaching beginners, they can forget how many individual steps were originally required.

The blind spot is especially common when coaches operate in the conscious-competence or unconscious-competence stages.

Because they can perform the skill successfully—sometimes without thinking about it—they may miss essential components when designing learning activities.

Successful athletes do not automatically become successful coaches.

Some former elite athletes struggle to explain skills that feel natural to them. Some coaches who were average athletes become excellent teachers because they remember the difficulties and stages of the learning process.

Athletic achievement and coaching ability are different skills.
## 47. Overcoming the Expert Blind Spot

The first strategy is detailed design.

Break the target skill down and list every technical, physical, perceptual, and decision-making component required for success.

The second strategy is seeking another opinion.

Ask another coach to review the learning activity. A less-experienced coach may be especially useful because they may still consciously think through steps that have become automatic for an expert.

The third strategy is asking a non-expert.

Explain the skill to someone unfamiliar with the sport. Their questions may reveal missing steps, unclear assumptions, or language that athletes will not understand.

The fourth strategy is recording yourself coaching.

Use video or audio to examine whether your instructions were clear, which steps you assumed athletes understood, how much you talked, whether athletes had opportunities to respond, and whether your feedback directed attention toward the most important component.

Effective coaches must reconstruct the skill from the learner’s perspective.
## 48. Combining Deliberate Practice with Specific Feedback

Deliberate practice requires appropriate feedback.

Without relevant information about performance, athletes cannot accurately identify the gap between their current execution and the target skill.

Feedback should be connected to a clear and realistic learning goal that is familiar to the athlete.

It should direct attention toward the most important component, provide information that can be used during the next attempt, and gradually help athletes become less dependent on the coach.

Feedback should not become continuous commentary.

It should serve the athlete’s learning rather than the coach’s need to speak or display expertise.
## 49. Augmented and Task-Intrinsic Feedback

Augmented feedback is external information supplied by the coach, video, data, or another observer.

Task-intrinsic feedback is the information athletes obtain naturally from what they see, feel, hear, and notice during performance.

Coaches should encourage athletes to use their intrinsic feedback as much as possible.

Augmented feedback should supplement what athletes can already perceive and direct attention toward important information they have missed.

A useful distinction is Push and Pull.

Push feedback provides external information and instruction.

Pull feedback asks athletes what they noticed and encourages subjective error estimation.

The coach should ask before telling whenever appropriate.
