# IN-SEASON — Athlete Development and Learning

## 26. Developing Athletic Talent and Skill

Athletic ability is influenced by many factors, including genetics, body structure, physical size, previous experience, and the quality of the athlete’s environment.

Coaches cannot control every influence on talent, but they can control the quality of training and learning opportunities they provide.

Successful coaches focus on the abilities that can be developed. They understand that talent is not simply something an athlete possesses. It is built gradually through purposeful practice, effective feedback, recovery, motivation, and long-term commitment.

The coach’s responsibility is to identify the athlete’s current level, recognize important performance gaps, and design experiences that promote continued development.
## 27. Deliberate Practice

Deliberate practice is purposeful training designed to close critical performance gaps and produce long-term improvement.

It should target a clearly identified weakness or developmental need, require complete concentration, demand significant mental or physical effort, and present an appropriate level of difficulty.

Deliberate practice should be challenging enough to stretch the athlete’s current abilities without becoming so difficult that meaningful learning becomes impossible.

Athletes need a clear and accurate mental image of the skill. Coaches can support this through demonstrations, concise verbal instructions, video examples, visual cues, and attention directed toward the most important parts of the movement.

Athletes also need opportunities to reflect on what they saw, felt, and learned. Coaches should reflect on the effectiveness of their teaching and change their approach when athletes are not learning.

Physically demanding deliberate practice must be balanced with sufficient recovery. Coaches should prefer shorter, focused periods of quality work and avoid measuring practice quality only by its duration.

The objective is not to accumulate the greatest number of repetitions. It is to complete the greatest number of focused, high-quality repetitions.

Improvement requires athletes to move beyond what already feels easy and familiar. However, leaving the comfort zone should be progressive, purposeful, and supported by feedback and recovery.
## 28. Promoting Long-Term Athlete Development

Long-term development places the athlete’s complete journey above immediate results.

The objective is to develop the physical skills, confidence, motivation, character, and understanding required for both high-level performance and lifelong participation in physical activity.

Coaches should avoid sacrificing future development for short-term success. Training should reflect the athlete’s age, maturity, experience, needs, and stage of development.

Young athletes should not be treated as smaller versions of adult athletes. Their programs should emphasize fundamental movement skills, enjoyment, varied experiences, and gradual progression.

Long-term development also means creating several possible pathways. Some athletes may pursue elite performance, while others participate for health, relationships, enjoyment, or personal achievement.

Both pathways are valuable.
## 29. The LTAD Framework

The Long-Term Athlete Development framework contains seven stages.

The first stage is Active Start. The objective is to develop basic human movements and positive habits of physical activity.

The second stage is FUNdamentals. Athletes develop fundamental movement skills through enjoyable and varied activities.

The third stage is Learn to Train. Athletes build a broad foundation of sport skills and begin learning basic rules, tactics, and strategies.

These first three stages establish physical literacy. The emphasis is on coordination, agility, balance, speed, strength, confidence, fundamental movement patterns, and enjoyment.

The fourth stage is Train to Train. Athletes begin following more structured and demanding training programs.

The fifth stage is Train to Compete. Preparation becomes more specialized and competition-specific.

The sixth stage is Train to Win. Athletes pursuing high performance attempt to maximize their physical, technical, tactical, mental, and emotional development.

The seventh stage is Active for Life. The objective is to remain physically active throughout life through competitive or recreational participation.

Participants may also contribute as coaches, officials, volunteers, or sport leaders.

The age ranges connected to these stages should be treated as developmental guidelines rather than rigid deadlines. Athletes grow and mature at different rates.

The central objective is to help as many people as possible remain active for life while giving those who choose high performance an appropriate pathway toward excellence.
## 30. Free Play and Diversification

Free play consists of spontaneous, enjoyable, and partly athlete-directed activities.

Athletes can experiment without constant correction, fear of failure, or responsibility for formal outcomes.

Regular opportunities for free play can increase enjoyment, encourage creativity, develop independent decision-making, give athletes ownership, and balance the demands of deliberate practice.

The coach should provide freedom appropriate to the athletes’ age, maturity, and ability to work safely without constant direction.

Diversification means giving young athletes opportunities to participate in several sports and play different positions before specializing.

Where practical, children can sample three or four sports before high school rather than committing too early to one activity.

Diversification develops a broad foundation of agility, balance, coordination, strength, tactical awareness, adaptability, and decision-making.

It may also reduce repetitive physical stress, overuse injuries, and emotional burnout.

Diversification can occur within one sport. Allowing children to play different positions gives them a more complete understanding of the game and helps them become versatile athletes.

The objective is not to create the best child athlete immediately. It is to build a healthy, capable, motivated person who can pursue excellence or remain active for life.
## 31. The Developmental Model of Sport Participation

The Developmental Model of Sport Participation emphasizes two central principles: free play and diversification.

Many high-level athletes developed in environments that combined structured training with frequent opportunities for spontaneous play.

Deliberate practice and free play serve different purposes.

Deliberate practice is structured, coach-designed, demanding, and focused on a specific performance gap.

Free play is flexible, athlete-directed, creative, and focused on enjoyment and exploration.

Both forms are valuable and should complement one another.

When deliberate practice is not balanced with opportunities for free play, athletes may face greater emotional exhaustion, reduced creativity, repetitive physical stress, and loss of enjoyment.

Sport sampling also allows young athletes to learn from different coaches, environments, rules, movement patterns, and tactical problems.

Lessons learned in one sport may transfer into another and make athletes more adaptable.
## 32. The American Development Model

The American Development Model is a five-stage framework designed to create healthy and sustainable sporting experiences throughout an athlete’s life.

Stage one is Discover, Learn, and Play, generally from birth to age twelve. The objective is to create a safe and enjoyable experience that develops fundamental movement skills and motivates children to return.

Stage two is Develop and Challenge, generally from ages ten to sixteen. Athletes begin developing more sport-specific skills, including individual and group tactics. Challenges increase gradually while broad athletic development remains important.

During stages one and two, participation in multiple sports should be encouraged.

Stage three is Train and Compete, generally from ages thirteen to nineteen. Athletes may reduce their participation to one or two sports and engage in more specialized training and demanding competition schedules.

Stage four begins around age fifteen and offers two pathways.

The first is Excel for High Performance, for athletes pursuing elite performance.

The second is Participate and Succeed, for athletes continuing through enjoyment, health, relationships, personal achievement, or meaningful competition.

Stage five is Thrive and Mentor. This is the lifelong stage. Participants may remain active, compete, coach, mentor, officiate, volunteer, or lead within the sport.

The age ranges overlap because development is individual. Coaches should consider maturity, ability, motivation, needs, and experience rather than progressing athletes according to age alone.
## 33. From Core Values to Coaching Strategy

Core values explain why we do what we do.

A coaching philosophy explains how we intend to do it.

Coaching strategies turn those beliefs into everyday action.

A long-term, athlete-centred program can be guided by three important values.

The first is developmentally appropriate programming. Training, competition, and recovery should match the athlete’s biological development, maturity, experience, and individual needs.

The second is equal opportunity. Every athlete should have a meaningful opportunity to participate, develop, and reach their potential.

The third is athlete-centred excellence. The program should be athlete-centred and coach-driven. Athletes’ needs remain central, while the coach accepts responsibility for designing, guiding, and protecting the developmental process.

Every coaching strategy should be grounded in a long-term, athlete-centred approach.

Immediate results should never undermine the athlete’s future development, well-being, or passion for the sport.
## 34. Optimizing Athlete Learning

Coaching effectiveness begins with the ability to teach.

Successful coaches view themselves as teachers. They do not evaluate teaching by how much information they presented, but by what athletes understood, retained, and could apply.

The principle is simple: you have not taught until they have learned.

When athletes fail to understand or improve, the coach should not automatically blame their effort, intelligence, or attitude.

The coach should reassess the explanation, demonstration, learning environment, activity, difficulty, feedback, and amount of practice.

The athlete’s learning—not the coach’s explanation—is the true measure of teaching.
## 35. The Fifteen Principles of Effective Pedagogy

The first principle is to plan. Define the learning objective and design the practice around what athletes should understand or perform by the end.

The second is to prepare the learner. Gain the athlete’s attention, explain why the skill matters, and connect new information to previous knowledge.

The third is to prepare a protected learning environment. Athletes should be physically and psychologically safe and able to ask questions, experiment, and make mistakes without humiliation.

The fourth is to present the task efficiently. Use short explanations, clear demonstrations, and simple cues.

The fifth is to pretest. Assess what athletes already know and can perform before selecting the starting point.

The sixth is to provide plenty of practice. Athletes need enough meaningful repetitions to understand, stabilize, and apply the skill.

The seventh is to make practice pertinent. Activities should be relevant to the athlete’s developmental needs and connected to competition.

The eighth is to make practice playful. Enjoyment, games, exploration, and appropriate challenges increase motivation.

The ninth is to make practice purposeful. Every activity should have a clear objective that athletes understand.

The tenth is to make practice progressive. Move gradually from simple to complex, familiar to unfamiliar, and controlled to competitive situations.

The eleventh is to make practice personalized. Adjust challenges, instructions, feedback, and expectations according to each athlete.

The twelfth is to pace the practice. Balance activity, explanation, recovery, and progression according to the quality of learning.

The thirteenth is to provide feedback. Feedback should be timely, specific, relevant, and actionable.

The fourteenth is to praise performance. Recognize genuine effort, improvement, effective decisions, and successful execution. Praise should be specific and deserved.

The fifteenth is to project poise, patience, and passion.

Poise means remaining calm, confident, composed, and dignified.

Patience means allowing athletes the time and mistakes required for learning.

Passion means demonstrating genuine enthusiasm for the sport and the athletes’ development.
