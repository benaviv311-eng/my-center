# IN-SEASON — Athlete Development and Learning

## 50. Promotion-Oriented and Change-Oriented Feedback

Promotion-oriented feedback identifies the correct or desired behaviour that athletes should continue performing.

For example, the coach might say that the athlete’s approach was balanced and the contact point was correctly positioned, and then ask the athlete to repeat it.

Change-oriented feedback identifies an error or undesirable behaviour that should be adjusted.

It should address the behaviour rather than judge the athlete.

It should be delivered in a calm and constructive tone, connected to a clear goal, and paired with possible solutions.

When appropriate, the athlete should be allowed to select a solution and try again.

Modern feedback should not be reduced to positive versus negative.

The more useful distinction is between identifying what should be repeated and identifying what should change.
## 51. Outcome and Performance Feedback

Outcome feedback describes what happened or the result of the attempt.

Examples include whether the ball landed in or out, how many serves reached the target, or the athlete’s time.

Athletes can often see the immediate result themselves, so repeating it may provide little additional information.

However, summarizing results across several repetitions can reveal useful patterns.

Performance feedback explains how the result was produced.

It may address the contact point, body position, timing, movement sequence, decision, or another important component.

Performance feedback is often more useful because it directs attention toward the factors responsible for the result.

It also helps athletes learn to identify and correct errors independently.
## 52. Delivering Oral and Visual Feedback

Oral feedback should be concise and focused.

Address only one or two important components at a time. Avoid overwhelming athletes with technical information.

Allow athletes to ask questions and ask them to repeat or paraphrase the feedback before trying again.

Athletes must learn how to listen for the central principle rather than attempting to remember every word.

Visual feedback can help athletes compare what they thought they did with what actually happened.

Video can highlight critical moments, compare attempts over time, demonstrate successful execution, and support athlete self-analysis.

Avoid repeatedly showing errors without presenting a solution or an effective model.

Feedback should always lead toward another meaningful attempt.
## 53. Timing and Frequency of Feedback

When uncertain, coaches should avoid unnecessary intervention.

More feedback does not automatically produce more learning.

Do not tell athletes what they can already see or feel. Provide the minimum amount of relevant information.

Avoid commenting after every attempt. Allow time for experimentation, reflection, and self-correction.

Athletes may want coach feedback on only a small proportion of attempts—sometimes around ten percent.

This should be treated as a reminder to avoid overcoaching, not as a fixed rule for every athlete or activity.

Feedback frequency should reflect task complexity, safety, the athlete’s needs, the practice objective, and the athlete’s ability to self-correct.

Frequent feedback may improve immediate practice performance while creating dependency and reducing long-term retention.

The objective is to provide enough information to support learning without preventing the athlete from solving the problem.
## 54. The Feedback Zone

The feedback zone is an acceptable performance range established according to the practice goal and the athlete’s current level.

When performance remains inside the zone, the coach can allow continued independent practice.

When performance moves outside the zone, the coach may provide feedback, ask a question, or adjust the activity.

As the athlete improves, the performance standard can be raised or narrowed.

Feedback frequency should not be determined by skill level alone.

The zone should reflect the individual athlete, team, task, learning goal, and safety requirements.

The feedback zone prevents the coach from reacting to every small imperfection and preserves the athlete’s opportunity to learn independently.
## 55. Delayed Feedback and Error Estimation

Instead of immediately correcting every mistake, allow the athlete to complete the attempt and consider what happened.

Ask what the athlete noticed, what they felt, and what they believe caused the result.

Allow another attempt when appropriate, and then add only the information the athlete could not identify independently.

For example, the coach might say: “I noticed that the ball travelled too high. What did you feel, and what do you think caused it?”

The coach may sometimes allow the mistake to occur several times before stopping the activity.

This gives the athlete enough information to recognize a pattern rather than reacting to one isolated attempt.

Delayed feedback and athlete error estimation can strengthen reflection, retention, and independent problem-solving.
## 56. Preventing Feedback Dependency

Athletes must be able to correct their performance during competition when immediate coaching is unavailable.

To reduce dependency, the coach should ask before telling, allow athletes to analyse several attempts, use questions to direct attention, gradually reduce unnecessary feedback, and encourage athletes to select possible solutions.

The coach should avoid becoming the athlete’s external control system.

The objective of feedback is not to make athletes dependent on the coach’s eyes.

It is to teach them how to observe, understand, and correct their own performance.
## 57. Athlete Ownership and Self-Correction

Athletes should receive meaningful ownership of the learning experience.

They should be taught how to identify performance errors, understand why they occurred, select possible solutions, and evaluate the next attempt.

This ability becomes especially important during competition, when the coach may be unable to provide immediate feedback.

Feedback should be distributed across both successful and unsuccessful attempts.

If coaches comment only on errors, athletes may learn to associate feedback with failure and overlook the information available in successful execution.

Begin feedback conversations by identifying the athlete’s strengths and what was performed effectively.

Then examine what should change and allow the athlete to participate in selecting the next action.

The final objective is an athlete who can think, adapt, and self-correct independently.
