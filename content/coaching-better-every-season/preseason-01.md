# PRESEASON — Purpose, Philosophy, and Team Culture

## 1. Defining Purpose and Core Values

Great coaches separate themselves from others by holding high expectations for their athletes—and even higher expectations for themselves.

A successful program begins with a clearly defined purpose and a stable set of core values. Winning should not be the program’s central purpose. Winning is a byproduct of how coaches and athletes approach their work, relationships, preparation, and daily choices.

The coach must first identify the program’s purpose and values and then create an environment that continuously teaches and reinforces them. Values become meaningful only when they are visible in everyday behaviour.

My purpose as a coach is to encourage children to leave the house, exercise, and develop values and habits that improve their lives. My goal is to help them remain active and healthy for as much of their lives as possible.

A clear purpose gives meaning to the coach’s work. It creates passion and supports the long-term commitment required to become an effective coach.

One core value is focusing on the next move. Athletes should always aim forward. They should not remain trapped by a previous mistake or become distracted by the final outcome. Their attention should return to the next action they can control.

Another value is that we do not whine. Coaches and athletes should not waste energy complaining or expecting the world to make them happy. They must accept responsibility, respond constructively, and become a positive force in their own lives and the lives of others.

We also work hard. Talent, circumstances, and opportunities are not sufficient without energy, effort, and determination. Athletes should practise and compete with the heart of a champion during every repetition, point, game, and match.

High standards must be combined with respect. The program should be demanding while treating every person with dignity. Athlete leaders should model these standards and help protect the team’s values.

The coach must learn to listen and see the person before the athlete. This means showing genuine interest in who athletes are, why they joined the team, why they train, and what they hope to achieve.

Athletes are more likely to adopt values when those values are relevant to their lives and connected to their motivations. Values should not feel like rules imposed by the coaching staff.

Core values can be instilled in four ways. First, define and communicate them repeatedly through conversations, meetings, statistics, feedback, and daily coaching behaviour. Second, create teaching tools such as videos, personal stories, former-player visits, and meaningful examples. Third, recruit athletes whose character fits the program and observe how they behave under pressure. Fourth, use fair and proportionate rewards and consequences to reinforce the standards.

Developing people contributes directly to performance. Communication, responsibility, leadership, and decision-making create more capable people—and ultimately better athletes.

Every sports program needs a visible and unifying purpose that reaches beyond winning and teaches lessons athletes can carry into the rest of their lives.
## 2. Converting Core Values into a Coaching Philosophy

A coaching philosophy describes how you will approach your athletes and guides your everyday decisions and actions. It should remain connected to your core values while evolving as you gain experience and work with different athletes.

Developing a philosophy begins with understanding who you are, both as a coach and as a person.

I coach to teach life-changing habits, including physical fitness, perseverance, discipline, and strong personal values.

My core values are perseverance, respect, honesty, passion, joy, generosity, care, and competitiveness. These values should influence how I communicate, teach, discipline, and respond under pressure.

I want athletes to feel that the team, its home, and its developmental process belong to them. They should act from a love of the sport, demonstrate solidarity with their teammates, and develop a genuine sense of belonging. They should feel like active participants in the team’s journey rather than people who simply follow instructions.

Athletic success includes developing self-discipline, perseverance, and healthy habits. It means showing up and working consistently, committing to the process regardless of the result, and knowing that you gave everything you had.

Success is not limited to winning. It is also reflected in the athlete’s character, effort, personal growth, and ability to remain committed during difficult periods.

Sport connects the body and mind. It teaches athletes to think beyond the immediate moment, believe in meaningful goals, and persevere throughout a difficult process. It also develops the ability to manage everyday challenges.

Self-esteem grows when athletes know they committed to the process, returned to the gym week after week, worked hard, and refused to give up.

My responsibility is to provide a safe and non-judgmental environment in which athletes can express who they are. I must believe in who they can become and always see the person before the athlete.

Discipline should begin with jointly established values, unity, and commitment to the team’s purpose. Clear rules and consequences remain necessary, but athletes should understand why the standards exist. Discipline should be supported by meaning and internal motivation rather than fear and external control.

My ethical standards require me to pursue success without stepping on others. I will earn loyalty through love and trust rather than fear. I will remain calm and clear-headed while acting assertively when necessary.

I will use my voice deliberately because constantly shouting removes the meaning from moments when increased intensity is genuinely required. I will work with passion and positive energy, elevate the people around me, and avoid judging or labelling athletes.

A strong philosophy must be supported by deep professional knowledge. Coaches should continually study their sport’s rules, traditions, values, strategies, techniques, and ethical standards.

A coaching philosophy becomes meaningful only when it consistently influences how the coach teaches, communicates, disciplines, and leads. It is not merely a written statement. It is the standard against which the coach’s daily behaviour should be measured.
## 3. Developing Coaching Effectiveness

Effective coaching should contribute to four areas of athlete development.

The first is competence: developing the technical, tactical, physical, and decision-making abilities required to compete effectively.

The second is confidence: building the self-belief needed to meet the demands of sport and everyday life.

The third is connection: developing the ability to create and sustain positive relationships with teammates, coaches, and others.

The fourth is character: encouraging ethical behaviour and qualities such as empathy, honesty, responsibility, and respect.

Success should therefore be evaluated through the athlete’s complete development—not only through competitive results.

Effective coaching requires three types of knowledge.

The first is knowledge of yourself. Coaches must understand their values, emotions, strengths, weaknesses, biases, and behavioural patterns. This requires regularly questioning their assumptions about themselves and others.

The second is knowledge of the athletes. Coaches should understand each athlete’s abilities, motivations, emotional needs, circumstances, maturity, and general well-being.

The third is knowledge of the sport. A deep understanding of technical, tactical, physical, ethical, and regulatory issues is essential for making effective decisions.

Coaches have six primary tasks: establishing a vision and strategy, shaping the performance environment, reading and reacting to what happens in practice and competition, building relationships, conducting practices and preparing athletes for competition, and learning through experience and reflection.

Pedagogical knowledge connects these responsibilities. It is the ability to transform professional knowledge into meaningful learning experiences. Knowing the sport is not enough; the coach must know how to teach it in ways that athletes can understand, practise, retain, and apply.

Coaches also require courage and commitment. They need the courage to examine their behaviour honestly and the commitment to ensure their daily actions remain aligned with their stated philosophy.

A coach is a teacher of both sport and life. Teaching should be evaluated through what athletes understand and can perform—not merely through what the coach explained.

You have not taught until they have learned.
## 4. The Athlete-Centred Coaching Approach

Athlete-centred coaching places the athlete’s long-term development and well-being at the heart of coaching decisions.

The coach still leads, establishes boundaries, and maintains high standards, but does so according to what will help athletes learn, develop, and gradually take responsibility for themselves.

Athlete-centred coaching does not mean allowing athletes to make every decision, avoiding discipline, or lowering standards. It means using authority to serve the athletes’ development rather than the coach’s ego, convenience, or desire for control.

The first principle is to see the person before the athlete. Coaches should understand the athlete’s identity, life, emotions, and needs beyond performance.

The second principle is to give athletes a meaningful voice. Athletes can participate in appropriate decisions about goals, learning methods, team standards, and personal development.

The third principle is individualization. Communication, feedback, challenges, and support should be adapted according to the athlete’s maturity, ability, confidence, and needs.

The fourth principle is developing independence. Athletes should learn to think, solve problems, evaluate their performance, and accept responsibility.

The fifth principle is explaining the purpose. Athletes should understand why they are performing a task instead of being expected to obey blindly.

The sixth principle is balancing performance and well-being. Competitive excellence should not come at the cost of the athlete’s physical or emotional health.

The seventh principle is shared accountability. Coaches and athletes should understand their different roles and commitments.

The eighth principle is measuring complete development. Success should include competence, confidence, connection, and character—not only wins and losses.

Before making an important decision, the coach can ask several questions. Does this decision support the athlete’s long-term development? Is it consistent with our purpose and values? Have I considered the athlete’s individual circumstances? Will it help the athlete learn and become more independent? Am I acting for the athlete’s benefit or for my own comfort and control? Would I make the same decision if the competitive result were removed?

Alignment between beliefs and actions is essential. A coach cannot claim to be athlete-centred while consistently making decisions based only on immediate results.
## 5. Keeping the Coaching Philosophy Fresh and Relevant

A coaching philosophy should provide stability without becoming rigid. Its central purpose and values may remain consistent, but its practical expression should evolve with the coach, the athletes, and the circumstances of each season.

Flexibility does not mean abandoning your values. It means adapting how those values are taught and applied so the philosophy remains relevant to the athletes you currently coach.

The coach should regularly examine whether the philosophy still reflects their beliefs, whether athletes understand it, and whether daily actions remain consistent with it.

A philosophy should not become a historical document that is written once and forgotten. It should remain a living guide that develops through experience, reflection, and honest conversation.
## 6. Using Critical Events to Shape the Coaching Philosophy

Important coaching incidents provide opportunities to examine and develop the coaching philosophy.

Critical events may reveal how the coach responds to pressure, conflict, failure, ethical dilemmas, role disputes, selection decisions, or unexpected changes.

Coaches should pause and reflect regularly. They should examine how they handled significant situations, discuss critical incidents openly with trusted coaching peers, and compare their actions with their stated beliefs.

Critical events should not automatically be classified as successes or failures. They are points for reflection that help coaches better understand their judgment, values, and behaviour.

The coaching philosophy can guide questions such as: Why did I respond that way? Was my response consistent with my values? How did my actions affect the athletes? What would I repeat? What would I change? Does my philosophy need to develop?

Reflection allows the coach to convert difficult experiences into deeper self-knowledge and a more mature philosophy.
