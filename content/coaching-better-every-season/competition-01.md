# COACHING DURING COMPETITION

## 98. Coaching Efficiently and Effectively on Game Day

Competition provides a moment of truth.

This is when you discover whether you made the most of your preparation and available time.

Develop a competition-day coaching approach that aligns with your values and personality.

A key question is when to intervene and when to observe silently, allowing athletes to work through challenges independently.
## 99. Strategies for Adjustments, Setbacks, and Conflicts

Ask yourself:

What strategies do I have in my coaching arsenal?

What team routines and rituals will be used before competition?

How will I communicate during breaks, timeouts, halftime, or between events?

What will I say?
## 100. Avoiding Over-Coaching During Competition

A common mistake is giving too many instructions or excessive feedback during competition.

This can overwhelm athletes and distract them from their performance.

Trust your athletes. You have prepared them to perform the skills and strategies practiced during training.

“Shut up more and let the players play” is an important principle.

Games belong to the players. Practice is the coach’s primary teaching time.

Championship coaches are not passive observers. They actively guide and support athletes without trying to control every action.

Three components define an effective approach to competition coaching:

1. Examine.
2. Encourage.
3. Educate.
## 101. Examine — Silent Observation

Observe athletes while they are in action and analyze the competition.

Be prepared to react and use your judgment, but avoid overreacting.

Sometimes doing nothing is better than intervening unnecessarily.

Restraint can be a great virtue in coaching.

Do not intervene too quickly.

Decide what to communicate, how to communicate it, and when to communicate it.

Observe how athletes respond to teammates, opponents, and changing competition conditions.
## 102. Listening to the Match

Listening to the Match: Observe your team’s performance and your opponent’s tendencies.

Anticipate tactical adjustments the opposing coach may make.

Watch players on both teams in a calm and objective manner, even under intense pressure.

Observe patterns of play and identify the contributions of individual players.

Determine whether your team is carrying out its game plan. If not, identify why.

Remain composed and decide what changes may improve performance.

Observe more and interrupt less.

You cannot see everything, so establish a clear focus for what you want to observe.

Prepare an observation strategy, identify key points, and establish key performance indicators.

Aim to spend approximately 80% of the competition observing rather than intervening.
## 103. The 80% Rule for Decision-Making

The 80% Rule for Decision-Making: If you have approximately 80% of the information you believe you need, that is usually enough to take action.
## 104. Encourage

Encourage: Two of the best words a coach can say to an athlete are “Well done.”

Do not praise only obvious outcomes such as scoring a goal or earning points.

Recognize productive off-the-ball actions, extra effort on defense, proper technique, mental toughness, and other behaviors that contribute to success.

Recognize the actions that create the play and the process that leads to the outcome.

Every athlete responds to positive encouragement and feedback.

Praise and encourage players who receive less attention.

Star players often receive significant praise, while teammates with less visible roles may be overlooked.

Direct encouragement toward athletes who do not play as much to keep them engaged and motivated.
## 105. Educate

Educate: Do not overload athletes.

Emphasize only a few important performance cues.

During competition, use the athlete’s name and deliver a short, clear, and repeatable message.

When possible, ask for acknowledgment to confirm that the athlete received and understood the message.

When you see an error, do not intervene immediately.

Give the athlete an opportunity to identify and correct the error independently.

Working through difficulties builds resilience and initiative.

Shouting instructions during the middle of the action is usually ineffective. Athletes may not hear the message or may be unable to process it while concentrating.

When pulling an athlete aside, use the same words, tone, and body language used when teaching the skill in practice.

Competition is not the time to introduce new concepts.

Before suggesting a change, ask: “Is this something we practiced?”

If the answer is yes, the adjustment may be appropriate.

Provide simple reminders that create reassurance and build trust.

Reinforce previously practiced skills and strategies.

When athletes are required to learn something new during competition, their performance may decline and lead to self-doubt or anxiety.

Because athletes are often highly alert when pressure increases, competition can be an effective time to educate through reinforcement.
## 106. Regulating Momentum and Making Adjustments

Regulating Momentum and Making Adjustments: A crucial part of the coach’s role is helping athletes remain focused and recover from failures and momentum shifts.

Momentum can feel like an invisible force that brings either positive or negative fortune to a team.

Teaching athletes how to recognize signs of momentum shifts is critical to competitive success.

If left unchecked, the perception of momentum can dramatically influence confidence and performance.

Choking may occur under intense pressure.

In team sports, one team’s collapse may become another team’s greatest comeback.

Coaches should develop strategies to help athletes recover from momentum swings.
## 107. Keeping Athletes in the Present Moment

Encourage athletes to focus on the next play, shift, point, or possession.

Direct their attention toward elements within their control:

- Effort.
- Technical execution.
- Concentration.
- Attitude.

In team sports, remind athletes to focus on their individual roles and assignments while trusting teammates to do the same.
## 108. Breaking Competition into Small Chunks

Divide competition into short periods and ask athletes to focus on the “game within the game.”

Athletes can maintain focus and intensity more easily for shorter periods.

Timeouts, tactical breaks, substitutions, and natural pauses can help athletes reset.

During the early stages of competition, athletes may show a lack of urgency because they assume they can increase their effort later.

Avoid this by setting short-term performance goals and emphasizing urgency from the beginning.
## 109. Acknowledge and Accept

Recognize and accept feelings of pressure and anxiety when they occur.

Do not fight the emotions.

Acknowledge them, then redirect attention toward the performance.
## 110. Center and Refocus

Control breathing and visualize successful performance.

Reconnect athletes with thoughts and images of their strengths and resilience.

Encourage the use of key words such as:

- Deep breath.
- Technical cue.
- Refocus.
## 111. Trust

When performance declines, remind athletes of the work they completed to prepare for important moments.

If athletes trust their preparation and their coach, they should also be able to trust their ability to manage the emotions experienced during competition.
## 112. Reconnecting with the Joy of Competition

Help athletes reconnect with the enjoyment of competing.

This can reduce pressure and fear of failure during important events.

The coach’s behavior directly influences athletes’ ability to remain focused and regain concentration when struggling.
## 113. Fudge, Fix, Focus

Fudge: Acknowledge the mistake and the resulting emotion, such as frustration or disappointment.

In this context, “fudge” is a mild expression meaning something similar to: “I made a mistake.”

Fix: Reflect briefly on what caused the failure and what could have been done differently.

Focus: Return attention to the immediate performance requirements and present competition demands.

In a fast-paced sport, the entire Fudge, Fix, Focus cycle may take only a few seconds.
## 114. Triggers of Confidence

Confidence may be strengthened by:

- Belief in ability and preparation.
- Sufficient physical and mental readiness.
- Positive attitudes and body language.
- High levels of concentration and energy.
- Trust in the competition plan.
- Focus on the process and fundamentals.
- Accepting and releasing mistakes.
- Successful performances.
- Mistakes or failures by the opponent.
- Negative body language from the opponent.
- Positive contributions by teammates.
- Successful tactical adjustments by the coach.
- Familiarity with the competition environment.
- Favorable officiating decisions.
## 115. Building or Regaining Positive Momentum

Create a detailed performance plan for each competition.

Teach athletes to visualize successful and positive actions.

Give clear and concise instructions during competition.

Provide frequent encouragement and positive reinforcement.

Keep athletes alert and ready to make tactical adjustments during important moments.

Share observations about the opponent’s tendencies and weaknesses.

Create a reset routine that emphasizes fundamentals and familiar routines.

Use trigger words to help athletes remain focused, play with intensity, and return to the present moment.

Remind athletes of previous successful performance experiences.
## 116. Calling Timeouts and Using Contingency Plans

Timeouts are an ideal time to help athletes refocus and control momentum swings.

Challenge athletes to solve performance issues independently.

Do not make them dependent on the coach.

Encourage players to communicate constantly so they can identify issues before they lead to major momentum shifts for the opponent.

Give athletes what they need most at that specific moment.

Rehearse what you intend to say before the competition.

Halftime is a longer pause.

Allow athletes to recover and provide only a few important points about game strategy.

Halftime is not primarily about inspirational quotations.

Meticulously prepare a bank of contingency plans that can be implemented immediately after a timeout.

Plans should include tactical responses and ways to respond to adversity.

In an away game, take additional time to plan.

Remind players of the home-team advantage and encourage them not to panic if the opponent builds positive momentum.

Do not rely only on contingency plans.

Provide athletes with simple, proven competition strategies they can use independently to regain focus.
## 117. Respond and Refocus in the Game

During moments of lost concentration, remind players to look around.

If one player is not focused, teammates can help bring them back into the game.

1. Check that everyone is ready for the next play.
2. Respond to the play.
3. Refocus between plays, make quick adjustments, and take responsibility for the next action.
## 118. Performing Post-Competition Activities

A big win often leads to celebration.

A tough loss often leads to frustration and anger.

The moments immediately after competition are emotionally charged and require special consideration.

One athlete’s big win is another athlete’s difficult loss.

Teach athletes how to behave after competition and model those behaviors yourself.

1. Count to ten, then shake hands, win or lose.
2. Discuss the match approximately 24 hours later, after emotions have cooled.
3. Some athletes want to relax after competition, while others want to train harder. Give them space to decide what they need.
