# OFF-SEASON — Reflection and Coach Development

## 141. Personal Mastery

“I have no special talent. I am only passionately curious.”

Personal mastery is not something you possess.

It is an ongoing process and a lifelong discipline.

Be proactive and never stop working to improve.

Focus on one thing at a time.

Direct attention toward the next opponent, next practice, next recruit, or next meaningful performance gap.
## 142. PofTSS — Personal Mastery Coaching Qualities

### Industriousness

Constantly work to improve.

Industrious coaches are energetic and proactive.

Focus on one thing at a time.

Direct energy toward meaningful performance gaps.

### Curiosity

Develop a deep desire to understand why, not only how.

Curious coaches view failure as fertilizer for deeper learning.

### Resourcefulness

Find or invent ways to overcome obstacles.

No two athletes or coaching dilemmas are exactly the same.

Approach each situation as though you are seeing it for the first time.

One of the great paradoxes of coaching is the ability to recognize patterns while suspending judgment and resisting the urge to act too quickly.

Take a fresh look at each situation.

A resourceful coach is relentless and does not rest until the issue has been satisfactorily resolved.

### Self-Examination

Maintain a desire to improve continuously.

Set aside time during the off-season for self-improvement.

Write a list of the areas you need to improve before the following season.
## 143. Knowing Yourself

Know your strengths, limitations, and potential.

Stay within yourself while recognizing that there may be no limit to what you can accomplish within those boundaries.

Do not be reckless in the pursuit of individual or team goals.

Do not be afraid to make mistakes.

Accept the paradoxes.

You can acknowledge limitations and still exceed expectations.

You can be disciplined and careful in your work habits while remaining fearless in the pursuit of your potential.

Self-knowledge combined with maximum effort is a powerful formula for success.

Moments of self-doubt will arise.

Challenges may also come from people who do not share the same commitment to personal mastery.
## 144. Growth Mindset — Improving Constantly

Earn your place every day, even if you are a professional.

The idea of a growth mindset is based on implicit theories of human ability.

There are two primary ways of thinking about ability:

1. Entity view.
2. Incremental view.

Entity View: Ability is viewed as natural, innate, fixed, and relatively stable.

Incremental View: Ability is viewed as flexible and capable of development through focused deliberate practice and resilience.

Athletes with a fixed mindset focus on proving their ability.

Athletes with a growth mindset focus on improving their ability.

Stay focused and continue learning.

Many external results are beyond your control.

Success means continuous learning and improvement.

| Criterion         | Fixed Mindset                                               | Growth Mindset                                                     |

| Intelligence      | Intelligence is static.                                     | Intelligence can be developed.                                     |

## 146. Learning from Setbacks

Learn from setbacks and build a bank of coaching strategies that can lead to sustainable sporting success.
## 147. Continuous Improvement

The drive to change and evolve sustains passion for coaching and keeps the coach fresh and excited about what comes next.

Never be content to rest on previous accomplishments.

Always strive to create a higher standard for your work and future.

Make a never-ending commitment to finding new ways to improve.

Avoid trying to fix too many things at once.

You cannot close every performance gap simultaneously.

“The hunter who chases two rabbits catches neither one.”

Focus on one performance gap at a time.

Continuous improvement means moving toward a desired state through uncertain territory while remaining sensitive and responsive to the actual conditions on the ground.

Keys to Continuous Improvement:

1. Engage in focused efforts to close meaningful performance gaps. Work on one area at a time.
2. Recognize that there is no clear or predictable path between current performance and the target performance level.

Continuous improvement requires awareness of the current situation, clarity about the desired performance level, and commitment to the next best step.
## 148. Critical Reflection

Critical Reflection: Critical reflection is not focused only on solving a specific problem.

It occurs when coaches examine their feelings, assumptions, beliefs, and general coaching philosophy.

Result: The coach reorganizes their mental model of coaching, leading to better decisions and improved coaching practice.
## 149. Reflective Practice and Critical Reflection

Reflective practice is the process of systematically thinking about and learning from experience in order to improve future actions.

Reflective practice and critical reflection are two distinct but interconnected learning processes.

Reflective practice is a form of single-loop learning. It focuses on identifying and addressing a specific performance gap.

Critical reflection is a form of double-loop learning. It examines the performance gap while also questioning the coach’s underlying philosophy, beliefs, assumptions, and overall approach.

Reflective practice improves specific actions, while critical reflection can transform the thinking that guides those actions.
## 150. Single-Loop and Double-Loop Learning

### Single-Loop Learning

Single-loop learning involves detecting and correcting errors without examining or changing the underlying governing values, assumptions, or beliefs.

It focuses on doing things right.

For example, a coach notices that practice is not effective and changes the drill, schedule, or instruction. However, the coach does not question the basic coaching philosophy, objectives, or definition of success.

### Double-Loop Learning

Double-loop learning goes one step further.

It involves questioning and potentially changing the assumptions, values, goals, or beliefs that guide coaching decisions.

It focuses on doing the right things.

For example, the coach not only changes the drill but also asks whether the practice goal, coaching approach, or definition of success is appropriate.

Single-loop learning improves actions within the existing framework.

Double-loop learning can transform the framework itself.

Double-loop learning is closely related to critical reflection because both require coaches to examine the mental models that shape their coaching practice.
## 151. Using a Systems Approach to Close Performance Gaps

A systems approach views performance gaps as interconnected pieces of a larger puzzle.

Every performance gap is influenced by underlying patterns, processes, relationships, and demands across the entire sports program.

A sports system is complex and adaptive. It cannot always be understood through simple cause-and-effect relationships.

Focusing too narrowly on an isolated issue can lead coaches toward the wrong solution, like trying to complete a puzzle without seeing the full picture.

Continuous improvement requires coaches to identify individual gaps while also considering how they connect to and are influenced by the wider performance system.

When examining a performance gap:

1. Pay attention to the specific situation and context.
2. Identify the main components of the system and the relationships between them.
3. Use multiple perspectives rather than viewing the situation from only one point of view.

A system is more than the sum of its individual parts.

After selecting a performance gap for off-season study, coaches should step back and examine it within the broader context of athletic performance in their sport and at their level of competition.
## 152. Closing Work Performance Gaps

### 1. List All Performance Issues

Identify every issue that may be limiting coaching effectiveness.

Do not attempt to solve everything immediately. The purpose of the first step is to see the complete picture.

### 2. Select Three Central Performance Gaps

Choose three high-impact gaps that are fundamental to improvement.

Prioritize issues that influence many other parts of the system. Even a small improvement in one central area may produce significant changes throughout the program.

### 3. Identify Growth-Limiting Factors

Examine the parts of the performance system that may affect the ability to close each gap.

These may include:

- Core values and coaching philosophy.
- Communication skills.
- Roles and responsibilities.
- Training and learning processes.
- Relationships and organizational culture.
- Available resources.
- Environmental demands.

Consider both the forest and the trees.

Understand the wider performance system while also examining its individual components.

### 4. Set Short- and Long-Term Goals

Establish specific and measurable goals for closing each selected performance gap.

Identify immediate actions, available resources, deadlines, and the desired long-term outcomes.

There should also be clear evidence that allows the coach to determine whether improvement has actually occurred.
## 153. Collaborate and Learn

“When one teaches, two learn.”

Coaches improve faster when they learn with and from other people.

The goal is not merely to collect more information. The goal is to become better at observing, questioning, adapting, and applying ideas within the appropriate context.

### Network One: Trusted Coaching Peers

Build a small network of coaches you respect and can speak with honestly.

The quality of the relationships is more important than the size of the network.

Choose people who are thoughtful, generous, curious, and willing to challenge your assumptions without turning every conversation into a competition.

Clarify the purpose of each relationship.

Determine whether you are looking for mentoring, problem-solving, observation, accountability, shared learning, or emotional support.

Schedule regular conversations instead of waiting until a crisis occurs.

A monthly conversation, occasional practice visit, shared video review, or post-season debrief can create a dependable rhythm of learning.

Be persistent.

Strong professional relationships develop through consistency, follow-through, and mutual investment.

### Network Two: Non-Coaching Professionals

A learning network should extend beyond coaching.

Teachers, athletic trainers, strength and conditioning coaches, psychologists, sport scientists, administrators, and professionals from other fields can provide different perspectives.

People outside the sport may notice assumptions that experienced coaches no longer see.

### Effective Mentoring

Effective mentoring requires clarity, persistence, empathy, and patience.

Ask the right questions at the right time.

Listen actively and attempt to understand before responding.

Learn through shared experiences rather than relying only on explanations.

Continue the learning process until there is clear evidence of improvement.
## 154. Building a Learning-Support System

Coach learning improves when coaches take the initiative to build and maintain a broad learning-support system.

This includes asking the right questions at the right time, learning through shared experiences, and continuing the process until there is clear evidence of improvement.

A strong learning-support system is critical for optimizing long-term coach development.
